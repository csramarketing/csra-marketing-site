What Should a Roofing Website Include to Turn Visitors Into Leads?

Written by Kemar Lawrence
Last updated on Oct. 21, 2026
The 7 things every roofing website needs to turn visitors into booked jobs.

I audit roofing websites every week, and the ones that actually book jobs all have the same seven things.

Not a redesign. Not a bigger budget. Just seven elements that turn a visitor into a caller, and most roofing sites I look at are missing at least five of them.

Get all seven right and your site stops being a brochure and starts booking calls. Work through the checklist below, or get the free website audit and I will score your site against it.

#01

Put a tap-to-call button where thumbs already are

Most roofing leads still start with a phone call, not a form. A homeowner with water coming through the ceiling is not filling out a contact form. They are calling the first roofer whose number they can tap.

Here is the deal: if your phone number disappears the moment a visitor scrolls past your header, you are losing those callers.

A sticky header or a floating call button keeps your number on screen on every scroll. It is a small change with an outsized effect, because it matches how people actually behave in an emergency. Why? Because a visitor who has to hunt for your number will not hunt. They will hit back and call your competitor.

The button should also show up on every page, not just the homepage. A visitor who lands on your roof repair page from Google should never have to go find your contact page to call you.

#02

Ask for 3 things, not 9

Every extra form field costs you completions. Conversion rate research consistently shows that shorter forms outperform longer ones. Trimming even one field is worth testing (typical finding across CRO research).

Your quote form needs three things: a name, a phone number, and a service address or zip code. That is it.

Anything else, the roof age, the square footage, how they heard about you, can wait until you call them back. A short form gets submitted. A long form gets abandoned.

Bottom line? The job of your form is to start a conversation, not to qualify a lead. Qualification happens on the call.

On a phone this matters even more. Typing on a small screen is work, and every field is a reason to quit. Three big, thumb-friendly fields will beat nine cramped ones every single time.

#03

Show your reviews before they go looking

Almost every homeowner checks reviews before calling a roofer. BrightLocal's 2025 Local Consumer Review Survey of 1,026 US adults found that 71% of consumers read online reviews regularly when browsing local businesses.

If your reviews live only on Google and nowhere on your site, you are making the visitor leave your website to find the proof. Some of them will not come back.

Put your star rating and two or three recent reviews on your homepage, right where the visitor lands. Real names, real jobs, real towns. That is what a nervous homeowner is scanning for.

Recency matters as much as the rating. A 5-star profile whose newest review is from two years ago reads as a company that stopped caring. A steady stream of recent reviews reads as a company that is busy and current. Key takeaway: Reviews are not decoration. They are the first thing a skeptical visitor looks for and the last thing they see before they decide to call.

#04

Photos of your trucks beat stock photos of models

Nobody believes a stock photo. When a homeowner sees a glossy model in a hard hat who has clearly never held a shingle, trust drops. They know it is not your crew.

Real photos do the opposite. Your trucks in a driveway. Your crew on a roof. A before and after from a street in their town.

Here is the part most roofers miss: photos are proof of existence. They tell the visitor you are a real company with real people, not a website with a phone number.

A gallery of ten real jobs will outperform a thousand words of "quality craftsmanship."

If you do not have photos yet, your phone is the camera. Take ten shots at your next job. Crew on the roof, the finished ridge, a wide shot from the street. That is more than enough to start.

Short video clips work even better. A 30-second walkaround of a finished roof, shot on a phone, tells a homeowner more about your work than any paragraph you could write. It does not need to be polished. It needs to be real.

#05

Put your license number next to the quote button

The biggest unspoken objection is "is this company legit?" A homeowner is about to hand a stranger thousands of dollars and access to their home. That fear is real.

Your license number, insurance mention, and any manufacturer certifications belong near every call to action, not buried on an About page nobody visits.

Why? Because trust signals work best at the moment of decision. A license badge sitting right under the "Get My Free Quote" button answers the objection exactly when it appears.

This costs you nothing and takes ten minutes to add.

Most roofers skip it because the license number is sitting in a drawer somewhere instead of on the website. Dig it out, put it in the footer of every page, and repeat it next to your main quote button.

#06

Build a page for every town you want jobs in

Homeowners search "roofing + their town," not just "roofing." If you serve six towns but your site only talks about one, you are invisible for the other five.

A service area page is a simple page for each town: the services you offer there, a few photos from jobs in that town, local reviews, and a clear way to call.

The catch? These pages only work if they are genuinely different from each other. Five pages with the town name swapped out and identical text everywhere else will not help you.

One honest page per town beats a generic "areas we serve" list every time.

If a homeowner in a neighboring town searches "roofer near me" and your site has nothing for them, that is a job that goes to whoever does.

Each page should answer the questions a homeowner in that specific town would ask. Mention the neighborhoods you work in. Show a review from someone in that town. If hail hit that area last spring, say so. Specificity is what makes the page believable, to visitors and to Google. Showing up for those town searches is one of the main jobs of SEO. #07

Make it load fast on a phone in a driveway

Most of your visitors are on a phone, standing outside their house. Roughly 60 to 75% of local business website visits come from phones (typical market range), and roofing skews even more mobile because the need is urgent and visual.

A site that takes forever to load on a phone loses the visitor before they see a single word. Speed research consistently shows that even a one-second slowdown shaves several percent off conversions (typical market range across speed studies).

The fix is not complicated: compressed images, no auto-playing video backgrounds, no bloated plugins. A fast, plain site beats a slow, pretty one every single time.

Here is a quick test. Open your site on your phone, on cellular data, not wifi. If you would not wait for it to load, neither will a homeowner.

The usual culprits are oversized photos and video backgrounds. A single uncompressed hero image can weigh more than the entire rest of the page. Compressing your images is often a one-afternoon fix that changes everything.

Pro tip: Test your site speed on your phone before you change anything, and screenshot the result. Make the fixes, then test again. Watching the number move is the fastest way to see what actually helped.

Before you go

Cut these three things before you do anything else

Three things actively hurt roofing websites. First, image sliders. Nobody watches past the first slide, and they slow the page down.

Second, stock photos, covered in #4. Third, jargon walls: "synergy," "solutions," paragraphs about your "commitment to excellence." Homeowners skim. They want to know three things: do you serve my town, can I trust you, and how do I get a quote.

Bottom line? Every element on your site should earn its place by answering one of those three questions. If it does not, cut it.

Frequently asked questions

How many pages should a roofing website have?

Enough to cover what you do and where you do it, and not one more. For most roofers that means a homepage, a services page or two (roof replacement, roof repair), a page per town served, an about page, a reviews page, and a contact page. Ten to fifteen solid pages beats fifty thin ones.

Should I list my prices on my roofing website?

Give ranges, not quotes. A homeowner wants to know if you are in their ballpark before they call. Stating that a full replacement typically runs in the five-figure range filters out tire-kickers without locking you into a number for a roof you have not seen.

Do I need a blog on my roofing website?

Not on day one. The seven must-haves above will move the needle far more than blog posts. Once the conversion basics are in place, educational content helps you rank for questions homeowners ask before they buy. Build the foundation first.

What is the single most important page on a roofing website?

The homepage, by a mile. It gets the most traffic and makes the first impression. Your phone number, quote form, reviews, and trust signals all need to be there. Service pages matter for search, but the homepage is where the decision happens.

Should my quote form ask for the property address?

Yes, a zip code or street address is one of the three fields worth asking for. It tells you instantly whether the lead is in your service area, which saves both of you time. Just do not add a fourth, fifth, and sixth field along with it.

How fast should my roofing website load?

Aim for under three seconds on a phone, on cellular data (typical guideline). That is the bar where most visitors stick around. Test it on your own phone with wifi off. If images are the culprit, compressing them is usually the fastest win.

Can I just use my Facebook page instead of a website?

You can, and you will lose jobs to roofers who have a site. A Facebook page cannot capture quote requests cleanly, does not rank on Google for "roofing + your town," and looks temporary next to a competitor with a real website. Social is a supplement, not a substitute.
