Why Your Roofing Website Is Not Converting (Even With Traffic)

#1 Your calls to action are invisible (or missing entirely)

Here is the most common problem I see. A homeowner lands on your page, likes what they read, and has no idea what to do next.

There is no button. Or the button says "Submit." Or it says "Learn more," which is the marketing equivalent of a shrug. A roofer's website has exactly two jobs: get the call or get the form fill. Every page needs a clear next step that says so in plain words.

Why? Because confused visitors do not convert. They leave.

The fix: One primary call to action per page, visible without scrolling, written like a human being. "Get my free inspection" beats "Submit" every time. Your phone number counts as a call to action, and it should sit in the header on every page, not just the contact page.

#2 Nobody trusts what they see

Put yourself in the homeowner's shoes. They are about to hand $12,000 to a stranger they found on Google. Your website is the only evidence they have that you are legitimate.

Now look at your site. Are there reviews? Photos of real jobs? A real address? A name and a face? Or is it stock photos of smiling families and a logo?

Bottom line? Trust is the currency of a high-ticket sale. A roofing site without proof is a brochure, not a salesperson.

The fix: Reviews on every page that matters, especially the homepage and service pages. Real project photos, not stock. Your license number, your service area spelled out, and a human face somewhere above the fold. BrightLocal's annual consumer survey has found year after year that review volume and recency drive who gets the call. Borrow that credibility and put it where visitors can see it.

#3 Your phone number is hiding

This one sounds too simple to be real. I have audited roofing sites where the phone number appears exactly once, in tiny text, at the bottom of the contact page.

Think about how your customers behave. A homeowner with a leaking roof is on their phone, probably standing in their driveway. They are not filling out your contact form. They are not navigating your menu. They want to tap a number and hear a voice.

Here is the uncomfortable math. In my experience auditing roofing sites, the majority of inquiries still start as phone calls, not form fills. Every extra tap between a visitor and your number costs you callers.

The fix: Your phone number goes in the header of every page, big enough to tap with a thumb. It goes in the footer too. It goes next to every form as an alternative. Make calling you the easiest thing a visitor can do, because for most of them, it is the only thing they want to do.

#4 Your site crawls on phones

Speed is a conversion issue before it is an SEO issue. A slow site does not just rank worse. It loses the visitor before your content even loads.

Google's own research found that more than half of mobile visits are abandoned when a page takes longer than three seconds to load. Read that again. Half your mobile traffic can vanish before they see a single word you wrote.

And most roofing traffic is mobile. Homeowners search from driveways, job sites, and kitchen tables, not desks.

The fix: This deserves its own deep dive, which is exactly what I wrote in my website speed guide. The short version: compress your images, cut the plugin pileup, and ditch the bloated theme. Speed is not a technical nice-to-have. It is a revenue lever.

#5 Your copy says nothing

Most roofing websites say the same five sentences every other roofer's site says. "Quality workmanship." "Serving the area since 2010." "Contact us for a free estimate."

A homeowner reading that learns nothing. Why you? Why now? What happens when I call? Your copy needs to answer the three questions every visitor is silently asking: Can you fix my problem? Can I trust you? What do I do next?

Why does this matter more than design? Because a beautiful site with empty copy is a beautiful empty storefront. Words do the selling.

The fix: Write like you talk on a sales call. Name the problem ("Storm damage? We handle the insurance paperwork."). Name the outcome ("Most replacements finished in one day."). End every page with one clear next step. Specific beats polished. If your cousin the English major would approve of your homepage copy, it is probably too vague to sell roofs.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my roofing website has a conversion problem?

Compare your traffic to your calls. A healthy roofing site typically turns 2 to 5 percent of visitors into leads. That is a typical range I see across the industry, not a published benchmark. If you are getting hundreds of visitors a month and single-digit inquiries, you have a conversion problem, not a traffic problem.

What is a good conversion rate for a roofing website?

For roofers, 2 to 5 percent of visitors becoming leads is a typical range I see across the industry. Emergency and storm-related pages often convert higher because the intent is urgent. If you are under 1 percent, start with the five fixes in this post.

Should I focus on getting more traffic or fixing conversions first?

Fix conversions first. Doubling your traffic at a 1 percent conversion rate gives you twice the trickle. Doubling your conversion rate gives you twice the leads from the traffic you already have, and it is usually cheaper and faster. Traffic amplifies whatever your conversion rate already is.

How much is low conversion costing my roofing company?

Here is an illustrative worked example. Say your site gets 500 visitors a month and converts 1 percent: 5 leads. At a 25 percent close rate (my working assumption for this example), that is roughly 1 job. At $4,800 gross profit per roof (a $12,000 job at 40 percent margin), that is about $4,800 a month. Lift conversion to 3 percent and you get 15 leads, roughly 4 jobs, and about $19,200 a month. Same traffic. The gap is $14,400 a month, and it is pure conversion.

Do I need to redesign my whole site to fix conversions?

Usually not. Most of the five killers are fixes, not rebuilds: a visible phone number, a real call to action, reviews on the page, faster images, sharper copy. A full rebuild makes sense when the underlying platform is the problem, which I covered in my WordPress vs. custom breakdown.

Why do visitors leave without calling even when my site looks good?

Because looking good and selling are different jobs. A pretty site with vague copy, no reviews, and a hidden phone number is a digital brochure. Visitors decide in seconds whether they trust you and know what to do. Design gets you the visit. Clarity and proof get you the call.

How fast should my roofing website load?

Aim for under three seconds on a phone. Google's research shows abandonment climbs steeply past that point. My full website speed guide walks through the fixes that matter most.
