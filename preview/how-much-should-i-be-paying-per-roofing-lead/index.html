Chapter 01

The lead price is the wrong number

Every lead seller quotes you a price per lead. That price is the number they want you to judge them by, and it is the wrong number.

Here is why. You do not get paid for leads. You get paid for roofs. A lead only matters if it turns into an estimate, and an estimate only matters if it turns into a signed job. So the number that decides whether you are getting ripped off is not the lead price. It is your cost per booked roofing job.

Cost per booked job is your lead price divided by your close rate. Buy a lead for $50 and close one in five, and that job cost you $250 in lead spend. Buy the same $50 lead and close one in ten, and the job cost you $500. Same lead, same price, double the cost. The lead price never moved.

That is the whole post in one line. Everything after this is the prices, the math, and the line where the deal turns bad.

Chapter 02

What the average cost per roofing lead looks like by type

Not all leads are the same product, and the price spreads are wide. These are typical market ranges based on publicly listed vendor pricing, not quotes, and they move by market and season. I have not tied them to a single study because no independent source tracks all four lead types in one place. Treat them as a starting frame, not a benchmark.

Shared leads run about $25 to $90. A shared lead is sold to three to five contractors at once. You are paying for contact information, not for the job, and four other roofers got the same name at the same time. The lead is cheap because the seller sold it five times.

Exclusive leads run about $100 to $250. One contractor gets the lead. You pay more because nobody else is racing you to the phone. Whether that is worth it depends on your close rate, which is the next chapter.

Live transfers run about $120 to $300. The homeowner is on the phone with you, live, when the lead arrives. The contact rate is the highest of any lead type because the person is already talking.

Booked appointments run about $175 to $200. The appointment is already on your calendar. You pay the most per lead and the least per booked job, because the show rate is high.

Then there is Google search ads. LocaliQ's 2025 benchmark of 3,211 US search campaigns puts the median search ad cost per lead at $228.15 for roofing and gutters, the highest of any home service, against $90.92 for home services overall. Roofing leads cost the most because roofing jobs cost the most, and because only 3.70% of roofing search clicks turn into leads. One caveat: LocaliQ sells ad management, so these are its own customers' numbers, medians labeled as averages. Directionally, the point stands. Roofing search leads are the priciest leads in home services.

So what should a roofer pay per lead? The honest answer is that there is no single right price. There is only a right price for your close rate and your average ticket. That is the next chapter.

Chapter 03

The math: how many leads it takes to book one roof

Here is the math, and it takes about two minutes once you know your close rate.

Step 1: count your close rate. Take the leads you bought last month and divide the jobs you booked by the leads you bought. If you bought 40 leads and booked 4 jobs, your close rate is 10%. If you do not know your close rate, you are already flying blind, and that is the first thing to fix.

Step 2: divide the lead price by the close rate. That is your cost per booked roofing job. A $50 lead at a 20% close rate is a $250 booked job. The same $50 lead at a 10% close rate is a $500 booked job. The lead price is the same. The business result is double.

Step 3: compare it to what one roof can carry. Work it off gross profit, not revenue. For a typical example: on a $12,000 roof at a 40% gross margin, you make $4,800 before marketing. If it costs you more than $4,800 in lead spend to book that roof, you lost money finding it. That is your ceiling.

Now run the LocaliQ median through that example. At $228 a lead, one roof in 10 leads costs you $2,280 to book. That sits well under the $4,800 ceiling. One roof in 21 leads costs you $4,788, right at the ceiling. Past 21 leads per roof at that price, finding the roof costs more than the roof pays you. The lead price never changed. Your close rate decided everything.

The bottom line: judge every lead channel by cost per booked job, never by cost per lead. A cheap lead with a terrible close rate is the most expensive lead you can buy.

Chapter 04

Three ways roofers get ripped off on leads

Getting ripped off rarely looks like a scam. It usually looks like three quiet leaks.

Leak one: you pay for dead leads. You are charged when the lead is delivered, not when you win the job. The homeowner who never answers the phone? You paid for them. The one who was only price checking with no plan to hire anyone? You paid for them too. If you never ask for credits on bad leads, you are paying full price for names that were never jobs.

Leak two: you race four other roofers on every lead. A shared lead goes to three to five contractors at once. The first one to reach the homeowner usually wins the conversation, and the other four paid for the privilege of losing. If your follow up is slow, shared leads are a tax on being second. Speed to the phone is not a nice extra. On shared leads, it is the whole game.

Leak three: you never run the math. Most roofers I talk to know their lead spend to the dollar and have no idea what one booked job costs them. Without that number, a lead seller can raise the price every quarter and you have no way to say no with confidence. The seller knows your ceiling. You should too.

None of these three is a reason to never buy leads. All three are reasons to buy them with your eyes open and your own numbers in hand.

Chapter 05

Your number: the ten minute check

You can find your number in ten minutes. Grab last month's numbers and answer four questions.

How much did you spend on leads? Add up every platform, every invoice, every subscription. Not just the per lead fees. The memberships and the minimum monthly spends count too.

How many leads did you get? Count every lead delivered, including the ones that never answered.

How many jobs did you book from them? Count signed jobs only, not estimates, not callbacks.

What is your average gross profit per roof? On a $12,000 roof at 40% margin, that is $4,800. Use your own ticket and your own margin.

Now divide spend by jobs booked. That is your cost per booked job. Compare it to your gross profit per roof. If the cost per booked job is under a quarter of your gross profit per roof, your lead buying is healthy. If it is over half, you are working for the lead seller. If it is anywhere near the full gross profit, stop and fix it before you buy another lead.

Write the number down. Check it every month. It is the only lead number that matters.

Chapter 06

When buying leads makes sense, and when it does not

Buying leads is not good or bad. It is a tool with a price, and the price has to fit the job.

Buying leads makes sense when you need work now. A new crew, a slow month, a storm that just hit your market. Leads fill the calendar this week. Your own website and your Google Business Profile fill it next quarter. Both have a place, and the fast one costs more per job.

Buying leads makes sense when your close rate is strong. If you close one in five shared leads, a $50 lead is a $250 booked job, and that math works on most roofs. If you close one in twenty, the same lead is a $1,000 booked job, and no lead price fixes that. Fix the follow up first, then buy the leads.

Buying leads does not make sense as your only channel. The moment you stop paying, the leads stop. Completely. You build nothing that keeps working when the spend stops. That is why the roofers who last build their own leads alongside the bought ones: a website that turns visitors into calls, a Google Business Profile that shows up in the map pack, and reviews that do the selling for you. Bought leads rent you a job. Owned leads pay you back for years.

So run your number, buy what the math supports, and build the channel that keeps working when you stop paying. That is how you stop wondering whether you are getting ripped off, and start knowing.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average cost per roofing lead?

On Google search ads, LocaliQ's 2025 benchmark puts the median at $228.15 for roofing and gutters, the highest of any home service. Shared leads from platforms typically run $25 to $90 and exclusive leads $100 to $250, based on typical market rates. Your market may vary. But the average cost per roofing lead is the wrong number to judge by. What matters is your cost per booked job: lead price divided by your close rate.

How much should a roofer pay per lead?

There is no single right price. Work backward from your average ticket. For a typical example, on a $12,000 roof at 40% gross margin, you make $4,800 before marketing. To illustrate: at 10 leads per roof, you can afford to pay about $480 per lead. At 20 leads per roof, about $240. Run the same math with your own close rate.

What is a good cost per booked roofing job?

A healthy range is under a quarter of your gross profit per roof. On a $4,800 gross profit roof, that is under $1,200 per booked job. Over half your gross profit per roof means the lead channel is eating the job. Near the full gross profit means stop and fix it before buying more leads.

Are shared roofing leads worth it?

Shared leads can work when your follow up is fast and your close rate is strong, because the lead price is low. They fail when you are slow to the phone, because three to five other roofers got the same name. Judge them by cost per booked job, not by the low sticker price.

Are exclusive roofing leads better than shared leads?

Exclusive leads cost more per lead, about $100 to $250 against $25 to $90 for shared, based on typical market rates. But nobody else is racing you to the phone. Whether they are better depends on your close rate. Run both through the cost per booked job math with your own numbers and let the math decide.

What is the difference between cost per lead and cost per acquisition?

Cost per lead is what you paid to receive one lead. Cost per acquisition is what you paid to win one customer. Cost per booked job sits between them. It is lead price divided by close rate. Cost per lead is the seller's number. Cost per booked job is your number.

How do I stop overpaying for roofing leads?

Four moves. Track your cost per booked job every month. Ask for credits on dead leads. Answer shared leads fast, because the first caller usually wins. And build your own lead channel, your website and your Google Business Profile, so you are not renting every job forever.

Should I stop buying leads and just do SEO?

Not overnight. Bought leads fill the calendar now, and SEO builds the channel that keeps working when you stop paying. The roofers who last do both: buy what the math supports today, and build the owned channel that lowers what you pay per job over time.
