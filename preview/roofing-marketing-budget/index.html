How Much Should a Roofing Company Spend on Marketing Per Month?

Written by Kemar Lawrence
Last updated on Oct. 23, 2026
The 5 to 10 percent rule: what a roofing marketing budget looks like in real dollars.

If you're doing $500K to $2M a year in roofing revenue, plan on spending 5 to 10 percent of it on marketing.

That range buys you a real website, steady SEO, and one paid channel working together instead of hoping the phone rings.

Below, I'll show you the exact dollar math for your revenue level, where each dollar goes, and a simple framework you can use Monday morning.

Chapter 01

The short answer: 5 to 10 percent of revenue

Here's the deal: Gartner's 2025 CMO Spend Survey found marketing budgets sitting at 7.7 percent of company revenue, and that survey covers hundreds of companies.

That's not a random number. The benchmarks for small business land in the same place: 5 to 10 percent of gross revenue, with 7 to 8 percent as the common midpoint.

Why does roofing land in that range? Because roofing is local, competitive, and high ticket. One job pays thousands in gross profit, but only if the homeowner finds you first. Referrals built your business. They won't scale it.

Most roofers I talk to spend far less than 5 percent, then wonder why February goes quiet. The budget isn't the problem. The absence of one is.

Chapter 02

What 7 percent looks like in real dollars

But here's where most owners get stuck: percentages feel abstract. Dollars feel real. So let's do the math.

A $500K roofing company at 7 percent spends $35,000 a year on marketing, or about $2,917 a month (worked example: $500,000 x 0.07 / 12). At 5 percent that's $2,083 a month. At 10 percent it's $4,167.

A $2M roofing company at 7 percent spends $140,000 a year, or about $11,667 a month (worked example: $2,000,000 x 0.07 / 12). The 5 percent floor is $8,333 a month. The 10 percent growth number is $16,667.

See the gap? The $500K roofer spending $800 a month on a lead subscription is underfunded by a factor of three. That's not a strategy problem. It's an arithmetic problem.

Chapter 03

Where the budget actually goes

Now the question I hear next: okay, but what do I spend it ON? Four buckets cover almost everything.

Bucket one: your website, amortized. A professional roofing website runs $2,000 to $5,000 as a one-time build (typical market range; I break down the full pricing in my roofing website cost guide). Say $3,500 spread over 36 months. That's about $97 a month (worked example). The cheapest line item you'll ever have.

Bucket two: SEO. A real local SEO campaign for a roofing company runs $1,500 to $5,000 a month (typical market range; full breakdown in my roofing SEO cost guide). This is your owned pipeline: rankings and reviews that keep producing after you stop paying.

Bucket three: paid ads. Roofing clicks are expensive because the jobs are valuable. Expect $30 to $80 per click in most markets (typical market range). At a 10 percent click-to-lead rate (illustrative), that's $300 to $800 per lead before you close a single one.

Bucket four: bought leads. Shared marketplace leads run $50 to $120 apiece (typical market range), and you're racing three or four other roofers to the phone.

Add it up for the $500K roofer: $97 (website) + $1,500 (SEO) + $1,000 (ads or leads) lands right around $2,600 a month, square inside the 5 to 10 percent band. The math checks out because the math was built from the budget, not the other way around.

Chapter 04

The "one bucket" mistake that kills pipelines

Now, the mistake I see constantly: an owner puts the entire budget into one bucket.

All ads. All lead buying. All SEO. One channel, one bet. And it works right up until it doesn't: ad costs spike, the lead marketplace raises prices, or Google shuffles the map pack and your one source dries up overnight.

Why? Because rented attention can be repriced at any time. You don't control the auction. You don't control the algorithm. The only channel you truly own is your website and the rankings and reviews attached to it.

I ran the full 24-month comparison of renting leads versus owning your marketing in my Angi versus own website breakdown. The short version: the renter pays forever and owns nothing at month 24. The owner has an asset that keeps producing.

The fix isn't complicated. Split the budget between owned and rented. Owned (website, SEO) compounds. Rented (ads, leads) fills the gap while the compounding kicks in. Never let one bucket hold everything. Next: the one ratio that tells you whether any of this is actually working.

Chapter 05

The only ratio that matters: what a job costs you versus what it pays you

So how do you know if any of it is actually working? One ratio: your cost to acquire a job versus the gross profit on that job.

The math I use on every estimate: a $12,000 roof at 40 percent gross margin leaves $4,800 in gross profit (worked example). That's the number your marketing has to beat. Not revenue. Profit.

Your customer acquisition cost is simple: monthly marketing spend divided by jobs booked that month (worked example framework). Spend $3,000 in a month and book 4 jobs, your CAC is $750 per job. Against $4,800 in gross profit, that's about 16 percent. Healthy.

My working guideline: keep CAC under 20 to 25 percent of gross profit per job (my rule of thumb, not a sourced benchmark). Under 20 percent and your marketing is an engine. Over 30 percent and you're working for your lead vendor instead of yourself.

Bottom line? If you don't know your CAC, you don't have a marketing budget. You have a marketing hope. Plug your real spend and your real close rate into the lead cost comparison tool and see where you land. Then check what a weak website is quietly costing you with the lost revenue calculator.

Chapter 06

The Monday-morning framework: a starting split you can actually use

One more thing: you need a way to split the budget without overthinking it. Here's the starting framework I give every owner:

40 percent to owned asset building. SEO, website improvements, review generation. This is the slow money that becomes the cheap money.

40 percent to paid demand right now. Google Ads or bought leads, whichever gives you the lower CAC this quarter. This is the fast money that keeps crews busy.

20 percent to test and learn. A new channel, a new offer, a seasonal push. Small bets that find your next winner.

That's a starting point, not a law (framework, not a sourced stat). Revisit it every quarter. If paid CAC climbs past your 25 percent line, shift money to owned. If you're booked out eight weeks, trim paid and bank the difference.

And never zero out a bucket entirely. A paused channel takes months to restart. Momentum is the most expensive thing to rebuild.

Chapter 07

When to spend more, and when to pull back

The percentage isn't fixed. It moves with your situation. Growth mode means spending toward 10 percent. New market, new crew, new service line? That's when marketing has the highest return, because every dollar is buying capacity you'll fill.

Maintenance mode means 5 percent keeps the pipeline warm. You're booked out, referrals are flowing, and you just need to stay visible. Trim paid ads if you're booked out, but never pull back on owned channels like SEO and reviews. Those are what keep you from starting from zero next February.

The one move to avoid: killing the budget in slow months. That's exactly when leads are cheapest to win and competitors go quiet. The roofer who markets through the slow season owns the busy one.

If you take one thing from this post, make it this: pick your percentage, fund it monthly like payroll, and measure it against gross profit per job. Do that for a year and the phone stops being a mystery.

Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage of revenue should a roofing company spend on marketing?

Plan on 5 to 10 percent of gross revenue. Gartner's 2025 CMO Spend Survey found marketing budgets at 7.7 percent of company revenue across hundreds of companies, which lines up with the 7 to 8 percent midpoint most small business benchmarks use. Growth mode pushes you toward 10 percent; maintenance mode can run near 5 percent.

How much should a small roofing company ($500K revenue) spend per month?

About $2,083 to $4,167 a month (worked example: $500,000 x 5 to 10 percent / 12). At the 7 percent midpoint that's roughly $2,917 a month, which covers a website (amortized), an entry-level SEO campaign, and a modest paid channel.

Should I spend more on marketing during the slow season?

Don't cut it. Slow months are when ad costs dip and competitors go quiet, so your dollars go further. Trim paid spend if cash is tight, but keep your owned channels (SEO, reviews, website) funded. Restarting a paused channel takes months.

Is it better to spend on Google Ads or SEO?

They do different jobs. Ads buy you jobs this month at $30 to $80 a click (typical market range). SEO builds a pipeline you own, usually $1,500 to $5,000 a month (typical market range), that keeps producing after you stop paying. Fund both: ads for now, SEO for later.

How do I know if my marketing budget is working?

Divide monthly spend by jobs booked that month. That's your customer acquisition cost. Keep it under 20 to 25 percent of your gross profit per job (my working guideline). A $12,000 roof at 40 percent margin leaves $4,800 in gross profit, so your CAC target is roughly $960 to $1,200 per job (worked example).

What is a good cost per lead for a roofing company?

It depends on the channel and whether the lead is exclusive. Shared marketplace leads run $50 to $120 apiece (typical market range), but you share them with competitors. Exclusive inbound leads from your own site cost more to generate and close at far higher rates. Judge leads by cost per booked job, not cost per lead.

How long until my marketing budget pays for itself?

Paid channels can produce in weeks. SEO typically takes 4 to 8 months to hit its stride (working guideline). That's why the framework splits the budget: paid demand covers the short term while owned assets compound. Expect the full budget to feel "worth it" inside 12 months if you're tracking CAC against gross profit.
