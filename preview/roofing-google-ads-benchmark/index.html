The Dabblers vs. The Operators: what the ads of 433 roofing firms actually look like.

Most roofers are not losing to "more competition." They are losing to a smaller group of operators who run ads like a system.

I know, because I counted. I audited 433 roofing firms across all 50 states inside Google's Ads Transparency Center and tallied the ads each one is actually running. 287 firms had a verified advertiser presence. 52 of them, just 18%, run 100 or more active ads. The other 82% split what is left, and 44% run fewer than 20 ads total.

Here is what that split means for you: the four tiers hiding inside it, what the top tier does differently, and the one gap that has nothing to do with budget. The full state by state dataset is free in the PDF, so you can find your own market in it.

Chapter 01

How I gathered this data (and what it cannot tell you)

I started with a simple question: if I line up roofing companies across the whole country, who is actually advertising on Google, and how hard?

So I researched 433 roofing firms, spread across all 50 states, and checked every one of them in the Google Ads Transparency Center. That is Google's public database of the ads an advertiser is running. For 287 firms, I could verify an advertiser presence and count the active ads tied to it. For 146, I could not confirm an advertiser under the firm's own name. Section 4 gets into what that means.

The 433 are not a greatest hits list. They are a working cross-section of the industry: national chains and franchise systems, big regionals, and single-market independents, in big metros and small ones. That mix matters, because the finding of this study is not really about any one firm. It is about how unevenly the industry shows up when a homeowner searches.

Three things about how I counted, because they decide how far this data goes:

Counts are advertiser entity totals. When a firm advertises under a parent company, a roll-up, or a franchise entity, the count belongs to that entity, and the firm is attributed to its home market state. That is how Google's database is built, so that is how the study reads it.

Ranges count at their low end. Where Google shows a bucket like 100 to 200 ads instead of an exact number, I recorded 100. Every total in this study is a floor, not a stretch.

Ad count is not ad spend. It is not impressions, clicks, or leads, either. A firm running 3 ads can outspend a firm running 100. What the count does measure is presence and testing: how much of the auction a firm shows up for, and how many messages it is actively rotating while it is there.

Hold onto that last one. It keeps every claim in this post honest, and it is also where the story gets interesting.

Cite this study

Read the shape of it, because the shape is the story.

The biggest single group is the Builders, at 38%. These are not beginners. A firm running 40 or 60 ads is spending real money and paying real attention. What most of them do not have is the machine around the ads, and Section 5 is about exactly that.

The bottom two tiers are 127 firms, 44% of everyone advertising. A handful of ads is not a campaign. It is a placeholder, the advertising equivalent of a yard sign. It keeps the name visible and changes almost nothing.

Look at the Testers for a second, because they tell on the rest. A firm running 2 or 3 ads almost always ran them once, usually around a storm or a slow month, and never built on it. The ads are still technically live. Nobody is home. When owners tell me "we tried Google Ads and it did not work," this tier is usually what they tried.

The top is a different sport. Across all 287 verified advertisers, I counted 24,061 active ads in total. The 52 Operators account for about 75% of them. The largest single entities in the study run 1,000 to 2,000 active ads. The typical verified advertiser, right in the middle of the pack, runs 25.

Why does this matter so much?

Because ads are an auction you enter over and over. Presence compounds. The firm in 200 auctions a month with rotating messages learns what homeowners respond to. The firm in 6 auctions with one message learns nothing, and pays for the lesson anyway.

Chapter 03

What the 52 Operators do that the other 235 mostly do not

You have met these firms, even if you have never heard the tier name. In most markets, one or two advertisers show up on every search that matters. After a storm, they are there. In the slow month, they are still there. That is not an accident of budget. It is a pattern, and the audit lets me describe it without guessing.

Here is what the top tier does that the other 82% mostly does not:

They cover the map of services, not one service. Separate ad groups for replacement, repair, storm damage, and gutters, and separate pushes for the towns they actually want. A homeowner searching "emergency roof repair" at 9 p.m. sees an ad about emergency roof repair, not a generic homepage headline.

They rotate and test. Hundreds of active ads means messages get swapped by season, by offer, and by what the numbers say. The count is the exhaust of a testing habit. Firms running 4 ads are not testing anything. They set it, and they forgot it.

They finish the click. Operator ads land on pages built for that search, in that city, with one clear next step. And the machine keeps going after the visit: their review profiles are current, and their reviews get answered. That last habit is worth money on its own. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey found that 80% of consumers say they are likely to use a business that responds to all of its reviews. Homeowners read those responses as a preview of how a company communicates when something goes wrong on their roof, and they choose accordingly.

They stay in the auction. Month after month, not just when the phones go quiet. Consistency is what turns an ad account into an asset instead of a lottery ticket.

None of this requires being a national chain. Most Operators are chains and big regionals, because scale forces systems. But the tier also holds independents who simply decided paid search was a core function of the business, not a line item to dabble in.

That last part deserves underlining. In a mid-size market, an independent running at Operator level is not outgunned by the chains. It owns its home auction outright, because the national players spread their systems across dozens of markets while the local Operator concentrates everything on one. The audit has firms exactly like that: single-market names sitting in the same tier as household brands. Scale is one way into this tier. Focus is another.

The point is not that these firms are smarter. They built the full system: ads, page, tracking, follow-up, and the reviews to back it up. Then they turned the volume up.

Chapter 04

The invisible third, and the gap that is not budget

Now the finding I did not expect when I started counting.

146 of the 433 firms, one in three, had no confirmable advertiser presence under their own name. Some of them are advertising anyway: through an agency account, under a parent brand, or on Local Services Ads, which sit outside the Transparency Center data. Some have paused. Some, near as I can tell, are simply not running search ads at all.

Whichever bucket they fall in, the result is the same. A homeowner searching for them by name finds nothing they control. A competitor researching the market concludes they are not playing. If your advertising runs under a name your customers do not know, you are paying to be invisible.

This is getting more common, not less. Every year, roll-ups and platforms buy strong local roofing brands and fold the advertising into the parent entity. The local name keeps its trucks, its crews, and its reputation, but in the auction it quietly disappears behind a corporate advertiser the homeowner has never heard of. The study counts those firms under the parent, because that is how Google sees them. Homeowners see them the same way.

The gap underneath is worse. I checked the contact setup on 186 of these firms, the part of the machine a lead hits first. 155 publish a named decision maker, which is good. But only 121 publish an email address at all, and when I verified those addresses, just 29 came back as clean, direct inboxes. The rest were role based addresses, catch-all domains, or dead ends.

Read that again. Firms spending money to be found have not finished the basics of being reachable. Every lead that lands in a dead inbox is a job that quietly goes to whoever answers.

That is the pattern under this whole study: paid traffic running into a weak website and a porous lead setup. The ads are the visible part. The leaks are behind them. I walk through what happens between the click and the call, and what each channel can actually carry, in my breakdown of ads vs SEO for roofing companies.

So no, the gap between the tiers is not just budget. A Dabbler with double the budget is still a Dabbler, just a louder one, until the setup behind the ads changes.

Chapter 05

Not an Operator yet? Build the machine before the volume

Here is the good news inside the data: in most markets, you are competing with one to three Operators, not an army. The tier is small. The gap is a build order, not a budget increase. If I were starting from the Dabbler tier tomorrow, this is the order I would build in.

1. The page. Every ad should land on a page built for that exact search and that city, with one clear next step. Speed is not a detail here. Google's mobile performance research found that 53% of mobile site visits are abandoned if a page takes longer than three seconds to load. A slow page taxes every click you buy, at full price.

2. The tracking. You should be able to name which campaigns produced booked jobs, not just leads. If you cannot tie a signed contract back to the ad that started it, you are budgeting blind. My lead cost comparison tool exists for exactly this math: what each channel really costs you per booked job.

3. The follow-up. Leads go cold at internet speed. In the lead response study published in Harvard Business Review, firms that contacted a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it as firms that waited even one hour longer. Your standard should be a first touch in minutes, automatic if it has to be, human right behind it.

4. The base. Ads rent visibility. SEO builds the asset that keeps the phone ringing when you are not paying per click. It is the same pattern I found across the 27 roofing marketing statistics: homeowners search, shortlist, and check reviews before they ever call. Own the ground they are standing on.

Put a price on standing still. One extra booked job a month. A $12,000 average roof. A 40% gross margin. That is $4,800 in gross profit a month, $57,600 a year (worked example, illustrative numbers). The Operators are not buying magic. They are buying that math, one month at a time.

Chapter 06

What this data cannot say, and what I am watching next

I would rather tell you what this study cannot do than oversell what it can.

It cannot tell you what anyone spends, what their cost per lead is, or whether the Operators are more profitable. Ad counts are a snapshot from October 2026, entity totals attributed to home markets, and plenty of real advertising, especially Local Services Ads, never appears in this data at all. Anyone who reads this study as a spend ranking is reading it wrong, including me if I ever write it that way.

Here is my prediction, in writing, so you can hold me to it. Within 18 months, Operators will be at least 22% of verified roofing advertisers, up from 18% today, as national chains and roll-ups keep pushing into mid-size markets. When I recount, I will publish the result either way.

Roofing Google Ads benchmark questions, answered

What is the roofing Google Ads benchmark?

It is this study. I researched 433 roofing firms across all 50 states and checked each one in Google's Ads Transparency Center, the public record of the ads an advertiser is running. 287 firms had a verified advertiser presence. I sorted those firms into four tiers by active ad count, from Testers running 1 to 4 ads up to Operators running 100 or more, so any roofing company can see where it stands.

How many roofing companies actually run Google Ads?

Of the 433 firms I researched, 287, about two in three, had a verified advertiser presence I could count. The other 146 had no confirmable presence under their own name. That does not mean they never advertise. Some run Local Services Ads, some advertise through an agency account or a parent brand, and some have paused. They are unconfirmed in this data, not proven inactive.

Does running more ads mean a company spends more on Google Ads?

No. Ad count measures presence and testing, not spend. A firm running 3 ads can outspend a firm running 100 if it bids harder on fewer searches. What a high count does show is coverage: more services, more towns, and more messages being rotated and tested at the same time.

What is an Operator in this study?

Operator is the name I gave the top tier: firms running 100 or more active Google Ads. There are 52 of them, 18% of the 287 verified advertisers, and they account for about 75% of all the ads counted in the study. Most are chains and large regionals, plus a smaller number of independents who run paid search as a core function.

Why do 44% of roofing advertisers run fewer than 20 ads?

The data cannot tell us why, only that they do. The pattern looks like testing the water or keeping a token presence: a few ads, one message, rarely revisited. 127 of the 287 verified advertisers sit in the Dabbler and Tester tiers combined. Whatever the reason, a handful of ads rarely covers enough searches to learn what works.

Will this benchmark be updated?

Yes. I will recount the tiers within 18 months and publish whether my prediction held: Operators reaching at least 22% of verified advertisers.
