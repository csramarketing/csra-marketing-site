Are HomeAdvisor roofing leads worth it for roofers in 2027?

Are HomeAdvisor roofing leads worth it for your roofing company? The honest answer is that the lead price is the wrong number to judge, and HomeAdvisor does not publish a roofing price at all.

Here is the deal. You are not buying a job. You are buying contact information that several other roofers bought at the same time, under a contract that renews itself, from a company the FTC already ordered to stop misleading contractors about its leads. In this post I will show you what HomeAdvisor leads actually cost, the math that turns that price into a cost per booked job, what the FTC record really says, and the contract fine print that catches roofers off guard. Then you can decide with your own numbers.

Chapter 01

First, HomeAdvisor is Angi now

If you are comparing HomeAdvisor and Angi like two separate choices, stop. They are the same company, and they share the same lead pool.

HomeAdvisor now runs inside Angi, under the Angi for Pros platform. A homeowner who fills out a form on one side lands in the same pool as a homeowner who fills out a form on the other. If you signed up with one, you are fishing in the same pond as the other.

That matters because every question in this post has one answer, not two. The price question, the worth it question, the contract question: the HomeAdvisor answer is the Angi answer. Anyone selling you HomeAdvisor as a separate channel from Angi is selling you the same leads twice.

Chapter 02

What HomeAdvisor leads actually cost

Here is the first problem. HomeAdvisor publishes no roofing lead price. None. A rep reviews the current lead prices when you call to join, and that is the only way to get a number. So before you spend a dollar, you cannot price the product. That alone should slow you down.

What do other roofers report paying? Contractor reports put it around $300 per roofing lead. Treat that as a directional signal, not a quote. Broader industry ranges put shared roofing leads at $40 to $110 per lead, with roofing replacement leads at $75 to $100 and up, based on typical market rates. Your market may vary.

The HomeAdvisor roofing lead cost question has no published answer, and that is the answer. A platform that will not publish its price wants you to commit before you can do the math. Do the math first, with the ranges above, and make the rep give you a number in writing before you sign anything.

One more cost sits outside the per lead fee. Angi charges an annual membership fee, the amount varies and the rep controls the number, plus, in many cases, a required minimum monthly spend. Add those to your lead spend when you run the math in the next chapter, or your numbers will lie to you.

Chapter 03

So are HomeAdvisor roofing leads worth it? The math decides, not the sales rep.

The lead price is the seller's number. Your number is the cost per booked job: lead price divided by your close rate. Run it before you judge HomeAdvisor at all.

Shared leads close at roughly 10 to 20 percent, based on contractor-reported ranges, because three to five contractors get the same homeowner at the same time and the fastest caller usually wins. Angi's own help pages say no more than five pros get one request. Take the middle of that range. At a 15 percent close rate, you need about seven leads to book one job.

Now run the prices through it. At $60 a lead and a 15 percent close rate, one booked job costs you $400 in lead spend. At $100 a lead, it costs you $667. At the owner reported $300 a lead, it costs you $2,000. Same close rate, same platform, wildly different business results, and the lead price alone told you none of it.

Compare that to what one roof can carry. On a $12,000 roof at a 40 percent gross margin, you make $4,800 before marketing. A $400 booked job sits well under that ceiling. A $2,000 booked job eats almost half of it before you buy a single shingle. And that is before the membership fee and the minimum monthly spend.

So are HomeAdvisor roofing leads worth it? The math decides, not the sales rep. If your close rate is strong and your ticket is big, the numbers can work. If your close rate is weak or your ticket is small, no lead price makes it work, because the price was never the problem. Your close rate was.

Chapter 04

The FTC record, in plain words

This is the part the sales rep will not mention, so here it is in plain words, from the FTC's own record.

In March 2022, the FTC filed a complaint against HomeAdvisor, Inc., a company affiliated with Angi. The complaint said that since at least mid-2014, HomeAdvisor made false, misleading, or unsubstantiated claims about the quality and the source of the leads it sold to service providers, including claims about how often those leads turned into jobs.

In January 2023, the FTC announced an order requiring HomeAdvisor to pay up to $7.2 million and to stop the deceptive marketing. The Commission approved the final consent order in April 2023. In November 2023, the FTC began returning more than $3 million to businesses that paid for HomeAdvisor memberships, mailing 110,372 checks.

Be precise about that $7.2 million figure, because it gets thrown around wrong. It is a ceiling on redress, not a fine that got collected. The order required $4,448,000 guaranteed up front, plus up to $2,752,000 more that was contingent. The money that went back to contractors was real, but the $7.2 million was the cap, not the check.

What does this mean for you in 2027? It means the company selling you leads has a public record of overstating lead quality and overstating how often leads became jobs. It does not mean every HomeAdvisor lead today is bad. It means you should verify everything the rep tells you, get the price in writing, and run your own math. The record is the reason the math matters.

Chapter 05

The contract fine print

The lead price is only half the deal. The contract is the other half, and this is where roofers get caught.

Twelve month contracts with automatic renewal. Angi typically runs twelve month contracts that renew themselves, with early termination penalties that can run to a third of what is left on the contract. Know your renewal date before you sign, or you will pay for a second year you never chose.

The spend target is a goal, not a cap. Your monthly spend target is a goal, not a hard limit. Instant Booking and Job Opportunity leads are charged outside the spend target, so your bill can run past the number you agreed to. Check your invoices against your target every month.

Lead credits expire fast. If a lead is bad, you have to ask for a credit, and you have 30 days to ask. After that, the money is gone. Most roofers never ask, which means they pay full price for leads that were never jobs. Put a weekly credit check on your calendar. After 30 days, the money is gone and there is nothing to argue.

You pay when the lead is delivered, not when you win the job. The homeowner who never answers the phone? You paid for them. The one who was only price checking? You paid for them too. That is the model, and it is written into the agreement you sign.

None of this is hidden. It is all in the contract. But nobody reads the contract until the bill surprises them. Read it before you sign.

Chapter 06

When HomeAdvisor roofing leads are worth it, and when they are not

HomeAdvisor leads are not a scam, and they are not a deal. They are a tool with a price, a contract, and a record, and they fit some roofers and not others.

They can work as a short term bridge. You need work this week, you have a crew to keep busy, a storm just hit your market. HomeAdvisor fills the calendar now. Your own website and your Google Business Profile fill it next quarter. Use the bridge, but do not live on it.

They can work when your close rate is strong and your ticket is big. At a 15 percent close rate on a $12,000 roof, even a $100 lead is a $667 booked job, and that math works. The bigger your ticket and the faster your follow up, the more room the numbers give you.

They do not work as your only channel. The moment you stop paying, the leads stop. Completely. You build nothing that keeps working when the spend stops, and you are renting every job from a company with the record in Chapter 4. That is why the roofers who stay in business build their own leads alongside the bought ones: a website that turns visitors into calls, a Google Business Profile that shows up in the map pack, and reviews that do the selling for you.

They do not work if you will not run the math. If you do not know your cost per booked job, you cannot tell whether HomeAdvisor is making you money or eating it. Get the price in writing, track the close rate, ask for your credits inside 30 days, and mark your renewal date on the day you sign.

So run your number, read the contract, and decide with your own math. That is how you answer whether HomeAdvisor roofing leads are worth it for your roofing company, and it is the only answer that counts.

Frequently asked questions

Are HomeAdvisor roofing leads worth it?

For some roofers, yes, as a short term bridge. The leads are shared with three to five contractors (Angi's own help pages say no more than five pros get one request), the close rate runs roughly 10 to 20 percent based on contractor-reported ranges, and the price is not published. Run your own math: lead price divided by your close rate is your cost per booked job. If that number fits inside your gross profit per roof, it can work. If it does not, no lead price fixes it.

How much do HomeAdvisor roofing leads cost?

HomeAdvisor publishes no roofing lead price. A rep reviews prices when you call to join. Contractor reports put it around $300 per roofing lead. Treat that as a directional signal, not a quote. Broader shared lead ranges run $40 to $110, based on typical market rates. Add the annual membership fee and any minimum monthly spend to your lead spend before you run the math.

Is HomeAdvisor the same as Angi?

Yes. HomeAdvisor now runs inside Angi, under the Angi for Pros platform, and both sides share the same lead pool. The HomeAdvisor answer and the Angi answer are the same answer.

What did the FTC order against HomeAdvisor say?

In January 2023, the FTC announced an order requiring HomeAdvisor to pay up to $7.2 million over deceptive marketing of its leads, with the final consent order approved in April 2023. The $7.2 million is a cap on redress, not a collected fine. In November 2023, the FTC returned more than $3 million to businesses, mailing 110,372 checks. Source: Federal Trade Commission.

Can I get a refund for a bad HomeAdvisor lead?

You can ask for a lead credit, but you have 30 days to ask, and the credit is not automatic. Put a weekly credit check on your calendar. After 30 days, the money is gone.

Can I cancel my HomeAdvisor contract?

Angi typically runs twelve month contracts with automatic renewal and early termination penalties. Know your renewal date and your termination terms before you sign, or you can end up paying for a year you never chose.

Should I stop buying HomeAdvisor leads?

Stop when the math stops working: when your cost per booked job eats too much of your gross profit per roof, or when you have your own lead channel bringing in work cheaper. Bought leads are a bridge. The goal is to own the channel that keeps working when you stop paying.

What is better than HomeAdvisor leads for roofers?

Leads from your own website and your Google Business Profile. The homeowner chose you specifically, so they close at higher rates, and the leads keep coming when you stop paying for them. That is the channel to build while HomeAdvisor fills the calendar.
