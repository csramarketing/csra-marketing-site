Chapter 01

One Formula Tells You Everything About Your Website

Here is the entire benchmark in one line: monthly visitors times your conversion rate equals your expected leads.

Everything else in this post is helping you fill in those two numbers honestly.

Your conversion rate is the percentage of visitors who take action: calling, filling out a form, or clicking a quote button. If 300 people visit your site in a month and 9 of them reach out, your conversion rate is 3 percent.

Most roofers have never calculated this number. They know roughly how many calls came in, but they have never divided it by their traffic. That single division tells you more about your website than any design opinion ever will.

Why? Because it separates two completely different problems. Low leads with low traffic means you need more visitors. Low leads with plenty of traffic means your site is wasting the visitors it already gets. The fix for each one is different, and guessing wrong costs you months.

Chapter 02

2 to 5 Percent: The Range That Means Your Site Is Working

For local roofing websites, a healthy conversion rate sits between 2 and 5 percent. That is my professional read from reviewing sites across the industry, not a published benchmark. Some sites do better, some worse, but if you are inside that band your site is doing its job.

Here is what pushes you toward the top or bottom of that range.

Sites near 2 percent usually have one thing in common: friction. The phone number is buried. The quote form asks for twelve fields. The mobile layout makes visitors pinch and zoom. Every extra step between "I need a roofer" and "I contacted this roofer" shaves points off your rate.

Sites near 5 percent remove every obstacle. Click-to-call button in the header on every page. A short form, not an interrogation. Reviews visible without scrolling. A headline that says what you do and where you do it, not something clever.

Bottom line? Your conversion rate is mostly a measure of how easy you make it to contact you. Fancy design barely moves it. Removing friction moves it a lot.

If your website was built to look impressive rather than to book jobs, that is usually where the gap lives. If you are thinking about a rebuild, here is how I think about roofing web design: the platform matters less than whether the site makes the phone ring.

Chapter 03

300 Visitors, 9 Leads, $14,400 a Month: The Small Market Math

Let me walk through three realistic scenarios so you can find the one that looks like your business. Every number below is an illustrative worked example, built on the typical ranges from the last chapter.

Scenario one: you serve a smaller market. Say your site gets about 300 visitors a month. That is normal for a roofer in a town of 30,000 to 50,000 people with modest search visibility.

At a 3 percent conversion rate, 300 visitors produce 9 leads a month.

Now convert those leads to jobs. Inbound website leads typically close at 25 to 35 percent for roofers. That is my professional read from industry patterns, not a published benchmark. At 30 percent, 9 leads become roughly 3 jobs.

Three jobs at $4,800 gross profit each (a $12,000 roof at 40 percent margin) is about $14,400 a month in gross profit from the website.

Here is the part most owners miss: that math works even at small traffic numbers. Nine leads a month sounds thin until you realize those three jobs cost you nothing per lead. A marketplace would charge you for each one of those calls.

Chapter 04

800 Visitors, 28 Leads: Where Most Established Roofers Live

Scenario two: a mid-size market. Your site pulls around 800 visitors a month. You rank decently in your city, your Google Business Profile sends people over, maybe you run a few service area pages.

At 3.5 percent, 800 visitors produce 28 leads a month.

At a 30 percent close rate, that is 8 to 9 booked jobs. At $4,800 gross profit per roof, you are looking at roughly $38,000 to $43,000 a month in gross profit attributable to the website.

This is the zone where most established roofing companies live, and it is also where the biggest hidden losses hide. If your site gets 800 visitors but only produces 12 leads, your conversion rate is 1.5 percent. You do not have a traffic problem. You have a website problem, and fixing it is worth roughly 16 extra leads a month without spending a dollar more on marketing.

That gap between what you get and what the math says you should get is the most expensive number on this page. Find it, and you have found your next project.

Chapter 05

Why Every Point of Conversion Rate Is Worth $29,000 a Month

Scenario three: a large or competitive metro. Your site gets 2,000 visitors a month. Strong rankings, multiple service areas, maybe some paid traffic mixed in.

At 4 percent, 2,000 visitors produce 80 leads a month.

At a 30 percent close rate, that is 24 jobs. At $4,800 gross profit each, that is roughly $115,000 a month in gross profit from website-driven leads.

Now here is why this scenario matters even if you are nowhere near it. At this scale, every single percentage point of conversion rate is worth 20 leads a month. Going from 3 percent to 4 percent puts nearly $29,000 a month in gross profit on the table. That is 20 extra leads, 6 extra jobs, zero extra ad spend.

Conversion rate is leverage. The bigger your traffic gets, the more every point of conversion rate is worth. That is why fixing your site usually beats buying more traffic. And it is why comparing Google Ads to SEO on cost per lead matters before you decide where to spend your next dollar.

Chapter 06

Two Numbers, Five Minutes: Diagnose Your Own Site

You need two numbers, and both are things you can check without any special tools.

First: your monthly visitors. Whatever analytics you use will show this. Use the last three months and average them, because roofing traffic is seasonal and one month can mislead you.

Second: your monthly leads from the website. Count form submissions plus calls that came from the site. If you do not track which calls came from the website versus other sources, that is itself a problem worth fixing, because you have no idea which of your marketing dollars are working.

Divide leads by visitors. That is your conversion rate. Compare it to the 2 to 5 percent typical range.

Here is what I mean in practice. Say you averaged 500 visitors and 10 leads over the last three months. That is a 2 percent conversion rate. You are at the bottom of the healthy range. Your site works, but it is leaving money on the table.

Now compare your traffic to your market. A roofer in a metro of a million people getting 200 visitors a month has a traffic problem first. A roofer in a small town getting 600 visitors a month has plenty of traffic and should focus entirely on conversion. If traffic is your bottleneck, this breakdown of local SEO for roofers is where to start.

Why does this distinction matter so much? Because the two problems have opposite solutions, and most roofers guess. Guessing wrong means spending six months fixing the wrong thing.

Chapter 07

Traffic Problem or Website Problem? Here Is How to Tell

Every underperforming roofing website falls into one of two buckets. Figuring out which bucket you are in tells you exactly what to do next.

Bucket one: not enough traffic. Your conversion rate is fine, maybe 3 or 4 percent, but only 150 people a month ever see the site. The site is not the problem. Visibility is. This is a rankings and local presence issue, and it is the slower one to fix because search visibility compounds over months, not days.

Bucket two: traffic that does not convert. You get 700 visitors a month and 8 leads. Your conversion rate is barely over 1 percent. More traffic will not save you. Every new visitor just bounces the same way. This is a website problem, and it is usually the faster one to fix because the changes are on pages you control.

The 30-second diagnosis

If your conversion rate is under 2 percent, start with the website. If it is 2 percent or better but your lead volume is still too low for your goals, start with traffic.

Most sites I review land in bucket two: traffic is there, but the phone number is buried three clicks deep, the form asks for ten required fields, and there is not a single review on the homepage. Traffic without conversion is just expensive window shopping. If that sounds like your site, here is why roofing websites get traffic but no calls, with the specific fixes for each problem.

The good news: bucket two is fixable without waiting on Google. The bad news: at 700 monthly visitors, every month you wait at 1 percent instead of 3 percent is roughly 14 leads you handed to whoever ranked below you.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good conversion rate for a roofing website?

Between 2 and 5 percent of visitors becoming leads is the range I see on healthy local roofing sites. That is my professional judgment, not a published benchmark. Under 2 percent usually signals a website problem: friction in the contact process, weak calls to action, or missing trust signals. Above 5 percent is excellent and usually means the site makes contacting you effortless.

How many visitors does my roofing site need to generate 20 leads a month?

Divide your lead goal by your conversion rate. At a 3 percent conversion rate, 20 leads requires roughly 670 visitors a month. That is an illustrative calculation, not a promise. If your conversion rate is higher, you need fewer visitors. This is why improving conversion is often cheaper than buying more traffic.

My roofing website gets traffic but no calls. What is wrong?

That is a conversion problem, not a traffic problem. The five most common causes are a buried phone number, a long complicated quote form, no visible reviews, slow mobile loading, and vague headline copy. I break down each one with specific fixes in my post on why roofing websites get traffic but no calls.

How long does it take for a new roofing website to start generating leads?

A brand new site with no search visibility can take several months to generate meaningful organic traffic. But a new site can convert paid or referral traffic from day one if the conversion fundamentals are right. The timeline question is really two questions: how fast can you get traffic, and how well does the site convert the traffic it gets.

Should I focus on getting more traffic or improving my website first?

Check your conversion rate first. If it is under 2 percent, fix the website before spending anything on traffic, because every new visitor will bounce the same way. If your conversion rate is healthy but lead volume is still too low, then traffic is the bottleneck and visibility work comes first.

How many website leads actually turn into roofing jobs?

Inbound website leads typically close at 25 to 35 percent for roofing companies. That is my professional read from industry patterns, not a published benchmark. Exclusive inbound calls close far better than shared marketplace leads. That difference in close rate is why a website lead is often worth two or three marketplace leads in gross profit, even if the marketplace charges less per call.

What should I do if my website is underperforming?

Start by running the math in this post: monthly visitors times your conversion rate, compared against the 2 to 5 percent range. That tells you whether you have a traffic problem or a conversion problem.
