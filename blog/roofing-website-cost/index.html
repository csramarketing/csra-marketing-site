Chapter 1

The short answer

Plan on $2,000 to $5,000 for a website that looks professional, loads fast on phones, and turns visitors into calls.

That range covers a proper build: custom design for a roofing company, real copywriting, the pages you need, and the technical foundations that let Google rank it. Below that range you are usually getting a template with your logo pasted on. Above it you are paying for custom strategy and design work that most roofing companies do not need.

There is one more number worth knowing: a single roof replacement runs $8,000 to $15,000. Measure the website against that number, not against the cheapest quote you can find.

Inside the sweet spot, what does the difference between $2,000 and $5,000 actually buy you? At the lower end you get a clean custom design, solid copy, and the essential pages: home, services, about, contact, and a handful of service area pages. At the upper end you get more of everything that moves the needle: a page for every town you serve, professionally written service pages instead of thin ones, review integration, photo galleries of your actual work, and conversion details like sticky call buttons and quote forms tuned for phones. Both are professional builds. The $5,000 version just gives Google and homeowners more reasons to choose you.

Chapter 2

What actually drives the price

Two roofing websites can look similar and cost very different amounts. These five factors explain the gap.

  • Custom design vs. template. A template is fast and cheap because a thousand other businesses use the same one. Custom design costs more because it is built around how your customers buy: big phone number, proof up top, quote form one tap away.
  • Number of pages. Every town you serve deserves its own page. A five page brochure site costs less than a fifteen page site with a page for each service area, and the fifteen page site is the one Google can actually rank in those towns.
  • Copywriting. This is the invisible line between a site that looks nice and a site that sells. Professional copy written in roofer language, organized around the homeowner's worries, is real work and it is priced like it.
  • SEO foundations. Fast load times, mobile first layout, proper headings, and the behind the scenes code that tells Google what each page is about. Skip this and you have a billboard in the desert: pretty, and nobody drives past it.
  • Booking and call setup. Click to call buttons that work on phones, a quote form that actually reaches you, and call tracking so you know which calls the website produced. This is the plumbing that turns visits into jobs.

Here's the rule: if a quote seems too cheap, one of these five was skipped. Ask which one before you sign anything.

And a website does not retire after one job. In my experience, a good site lasts three to five years before it needs a real refresh. That is the return almost no other marketing spend in roofing can touch.

Now compare it to buying leads. Roofing leads from shared lead marketplaces typically run $50 to $100 or more per lead (typical market range, varies by platform), and shared means three of your competitors got the same name. Ten leads a month at $75 each is $750 a month, $9,000 a year, forever, for leads you do not own. A website builds an asset that produces leads you DO own, at a cost per lead that drops every month you have it.

The break-even question is not whether you can afford a website. It is how many months of bought leads it takes to exceed the cost of owning the asset. For most roofers I talk to, the answer is under a year. After that, your website is the cheapest salesperson you have ever hired, and it never asks for a raise.

Chapter 4

Why cheap websites cost roofers jobs

The cheapest website is the one that looks fine and quietly sends your calls to a competitor. These are the leaks I see most.

  • Slow on phones. Most homeowners search on a phone, often standing in a driveway staring at a leak. If your site takes more than a few seconds to load, they hit back and call whoever loaded faster.
  • No click to call. A phone number buried in the footer is not a call button. Every extra tap between a homeowner and your number costs you calls.
  • Looks dated. A roof is one of the biggest checks a homeowner writes. An outdated site makes them wonder what else about your company is outdated.
  • No service area pages. If you serve six towns but your site only mentions one, Google has little reason to show you in the other five. Each town needs its own page.
  • No proof. No reviews, no photos of finished roofs, no trust signals. Homeowners compare you side by side with competitors in seconds, and proof is what they compare.
  • Contact info is hard to find. It sounds basic because it is. If a homeowner cannot find how to reach you in five seconds, your site has failed its one job.

Chapter 5

Template vs. custom vs. AI-built: an honest breakdown

Every roofing website falls into one of three buckets. Each has a real price range and a real tradeoff. Here is the honest version.

Bucket one is the template site, $500 to $1,500. Your designer picks a pre-made roofing template, drops in your logo and phone number, and hands it over in a week. It looks fine. The problem is that three other roofers in your market bought the same template. Homeowners cannot tell you apart, and Google has seen the same layout a thousand times. Templates are fine if you just need to exist online. They are not fine if you need to win.

Bucket two is the custom build, $3,000 to $10,000 or more. Everything is designed around your company: your services, your towns, your reviews, your photos. It takes longer, usually three to six weeks, because real work is happening. The payoff is a site that actually converts visitors into estimate calls instead of just sitting there. For most established roofing companies, this is where the smart money goes.

Bucket three is the new one: AI-assisted builds. The designer uses AI tools to move faster on layout, copy drafts, and structure, then a human refines everything. Turnaround drops to days instead of weeks, and prices often land between template and custom. The quality depends entirely on the human doing the refining. A skilled designer with AI is faster. An unskilled one with AI is just faster at making generic sites.

Here is what actually matters, and it is not which bucket you pick. It is whether the finished site books jobs. A $1,200 template that ranks and converts beats an $8,000 custom site that nobody finds. Judge the result, not the method. Ask every designer the same question: show me a roofing site you built and tell me what it did for their call volume. The ones who can answer are the ones worth paying.

Chapter 6

The hidden costs nobody puts in the quote

The number on the proposal is not the whole number. Every roofing website has a second price tag, the cost of keeping it alive, and most roofers never hear about it until the first renewal email lands.

Start with the domain. That is the www.yourcompany.com address, and it runs about $15 a year. Cheap, but it has to be registered in YOUR name. If your designer registers it under their account, you do not really own your web address. Ask for the login on day one.

Then hosting. This is the rented space where your website lives. Basic shared hosting typically runs $10 to $30 a month. Managed hosting, where someone else handles speed, security, and backups, runs $30 to $80 a month. Either way, somebody pays it every month, forever. A website with lapsed hosting is a website that does not exist.

SSL is the little padlock in the browser bar. It used to cost extra. Today any decent host includes it free, and Google penalizes sites that do not have it. If a designer tries to charge you a separate SSL fee in 2027, that tells you everything about how current they are.

Content updates are the quiet budget killer. Your services change, your service area expands, you want to add storm damage pages before hail season. Some designers include a few updates a month. Others bill hourly, typically $75 to $150 an hour (market range), and a "quick change" somehow takes two hours. Get the update policy in writing before you sign.

Add it all up and the honest math looks like this. A $3,000 website really costs $3,000 up front plus $300 to $800 a year to keep it running and fresh. That is still cheap compared to one roofing job. But you should know the full number before you commit, not after.

Chapter 7

Should you just build it yourself?

Sometimes yes. Here is the honest breakdown.

DIY makes sense when you need a simple placeholder so you exist online, the budget is genuinely tight, and you are comfortable with tech. A basic one-page site with your number and service area beats no site at all.

Hire when you want calls from Google, you are competing for the map pack in your towns, or your time is better spent on roofs. Your hourly rate on a job is higher than any designer's. Every weekend you spend fighting a website builder is a weekend you are not selling work.

The middle path I see most roofers take: hire the build once, done right, then handle small updates yourself. You get the professional foundation without an ongoing bill for things you can do in five minutes.

Chapter 8

Seven questions to ask before you pay a dollar

Most roofers hire a web designer the way they hire a subcontractor: handshake, gut feeling, hope. A website is not a shingle delivery. Ask these seven questions before money changes hands.

  • Do I own my website and domain outright? The only acceptable answer is yes, in writing. If the designer keeps ownership and licenses the site to you, you are renting your own business asset.
  • What happens if I want to leave? Can you take the site to another host? Is there an exit fee? A good designer answers this easily because they have nothing to hide.
  • Who writes the content? You know roofing, they know websites. Somebody has to write the service pages, and "we will figure it out" means thin, generic copy that ranks for nothing. Make sure real writing is in the scope.
  • Is SEO included or extra? A beautiful site that Google cannot find is a billboard in the desert. Know exactly what search optimization comes with the build versus what costs more later. My web design for roofers page lays out how a proper build answers each of these.
  • What is the real timeline? Not the sales timeline, the real one. Ask what they need from you and when, because most delays come down to you not sending photos and logo files. A designer who tells you this upfront is being straight with you.
  • Can I see roofing sites you have built? Not mockups, live sites. Visit them on your phone. If they load slow or look broken on mobile, walk away. Most of your customers will meet you on a phone screen first.
  • What does support look like after launch? Thirty days of fixes? A monthly plan? Nothing? The site will need attention. Know the arrangement before launch day, not after something breaks.

A designer who answers all seven clearly is rare and worth every penny. One who dodges them is telling you exactly how the project will go.

Chapter 9

Red flags: how roofers get burned

Talk to enough roofers and you hear the same horror stories. Here is how to spot them before they happen to you.

The hostage site is the big one. The designer builds your site on their own proprietary system, registers everything under their name, and charges you a monthly fee to keep it online. Stop paying and your website vanishes. You never owned it. Always ask who owns the domain, the hosting account, and the site files. Get it in the contract.

The disappearing freelancer is next. Great price, great portfolio, then they vanish mid-project with your deposit. Protect yourself the boring way: pay in milestones, never more than 50 percent up front, and get a written timeline with deliverables.

Watch for the $499 SEO upsell. Real search optimization is ongoing work: content, reviews strategy, local listings, links. Anyone selling it as a one-time $499 add-on is selling you a report you will never read. SEO is either a real monthly effort or it is theater. My local SEO for roofers page shows what the real version looks like month to month.

No contract means no recourse. If a designer will not put scope, timeline, ownership, and payment terms in writing, they are not a professional. Walk away no matter how good the price sounds.

And the portfolio full of mockups. Pretty pictures of websites that were never built, never launched, never had to actually perform. Insist on live URLs. Click them. Load them on your phone. A designer is only as good as the sites surviving contact with real customers.

Roofers get burned the same way homeowners get burned by storm chasers: speed, pressure, and no paper trail. Slow down, ask the questions from Chapter 8, and you will be fine.

  • First, know your number. Pull up your average job value. If one replacement is worth $10,000 to you, any website under $5,000 that brings even one extra job a year is a no-brainer. Write that number down. It ends every pricing debate.

  • Third, talk to one designer. Not five. One good one. Ask the seven questions from Chapter 8 and watch how they answer. You will know in fifteen minutes whether they have done this before.
  • Fourth, stop buying leads you do not own. Every dollar that goes to a lead marketplace is rent. A website is ownership. Shift the budget as fast as you responsibly can.

Quick answers

The questions roofing owners ask me most about website cost.

How much does a roofing website cost?
Most roofing companies invest $2,000 to $5,000 for a professional build: custom design, real copywriting, SEO foundations, and mobile speed. DIY builders run $0 to $300, budget freelancers $800 to $2,500, and large custom agency projects $5,000 to $10,000 or more.
How long does it take to build?
A template site can be live in a week. A proper custom build takes three to six weeks, and most of the delay is waiting on you for photos, logo files, and content approval. AI-assisted builds often land in the one to two week range. Anyone promising a full custom site in 48 hours is selling you a template with your name on it.
Do I need SEO too, or is the website enough?
The website is the foundation; SEO is what gets it found. A website with no SEO is like a beautiful office with no sign on the door. Get the website right first, then layer SEO on top. Doing it in reverse order is like advertising a phone number that goes to voicemail.
Will a new website actually get me more calls?
If your current site is slow, dated, or invisible on Google, yes, and the math is simple: one extra roof replacement at $8,000 to $15,000 pays for a professional build several times over. The fastest way to find out is a website audit, which shows exactly where your calls are leaking today.
Should I build it myself with a drag-and-drop builder?
You can, and it beats having nothing. But DIY sites almost always stall at thin content, no local SEO structure, and a contact form nobody monitors. If your time is better spent on roofs and estimates, hiring the build usually costs less in lost jobs than DIY saves in fees.
What are the hidden costs of a roofing website?
Beyond the build price, budget for the domain (about $15 a year), hosting ($10 to $80 a month depending on the setup), and content updates ($75 to $150 an hour if your designer bills that way). A $3,000 website realistically costs $300 to $800 a year to keep running and fresh.
Do I own my website if a designer builds it?
You should, but only if it is in writing. Make sure you own the domain registration, the hosting account, and the site files outright. If the designer keeps ownership and charges you monthly to keep the site online, you are renting your own business asset.
What if I already have a website but it brings zero jobs?
You probably do not need a new website. You need a diagnosis. Most underperforming roofing sites fail for the same three reasons: they do not rank where homeowners search, they load too slowly on phones, or they never clearly ask the visitor to call. A proper audit finds which one is killing you, and the fix is often smaller and cheaper than a rebuild.
